Estimated reading time: 10 minutes

Throughout this handbook we have leaned on one dataset, the longitudinal HIV antiretroviral therapy cohort (Wal and Geskus 2011), to illustrate every major concept, from defining a causal question to diagnosing how an estimator behaves (Petersen and Laan 2014; Dang, Tager, and Petersen 2023; Hernán and Robins 2025). This closing section gathers the lessons and follows the path from naive methods to principled causal estimation. For the full data description, including the variable dictionary and target trial protocol, see the Running Case Study.

25.1 What a naive analysis might have done

Without the roadmap, a researcher would likely fit a Cox or pooled logistic regression, adjust for time-varying CD4 because it is a confounder, and report the hazard ratio as the effect of HAART. Two of those moves are the traps we developed earlier in the book. Adjusting for CD4 blocks part of the treatment effect, since CD4 is itself affected by prior treatment (Chapter 12), and the hazard ratio answers a different question than the one a clinician asks (Chapter 4). As the running case study shows, the upshot can make an effective treatment look harmful.

25.2 How the Causal Roadmap clarified the question

Running the seven steps of Chapter 5 on this cohort fixed each of those problems before any model was fit. A target trial protocol set the question: the effect on mortality of initiating HAART at seroconversion versus not. A DAG made the dual role of CD4 explicit, a confounder of future treatment and a mediator of past treatment. The estimand was the marginal risk difference, not a hazard ratio. And the identification assumptions (exchangeability, consistency, positivity) were stated alongside where they might fail, namely unmeasured adherence and clinical staging. Only then were g-computation, IPTW, and TMLE (Laan and Rubin 2006) applied and compared, using Super Learner (Laan, Polley, and Hubbard 2007) for flexible nuisance estimation.

25.3 Why time-dependent confounding required longitudinal methods

The central lesson of this case study is that standard regression cannot handle treatment-confounder feedback. When a time-varying confounder (cd4.sqrt) is both a consequence of prior treatment (haartind) and a cause of future treatment, the analyst faces an impossible choice in a regression framework:

  • Do not adjust for time-varying CD4: leaves confounding of future treatment unaddressed.
  • Adjust for time-varying CD4: blocks part of the treatment effect and introduces collider bias.

Neither option produces a consistent estimate of the causal effect, as Chapter 12 demonstrates concretely. The g-methods (g-computation, IPTW with marginal structural models, longitudinal TMLE) escape the dilemma by modeling the treatment mechanism separately from the outcome and then using that model to reweight or standardize the analysis. Chapter 13 walked through the mechanics of longitudinal TMLE step by step: backwards sequential regression conditions on cd4.sqrt where it confounds future treatment and integrates it out where it mediates past treatment. This is not a minor technical improvement. It changes what can be estimated at all.

25.4 What the example illustrates about real-world evidence analyses

The HIV/HAART example is not unique. Treatment-confounder feedback arises whenever:

  • Treatment decisions respond to time-varying patient status (lab values, symptoms, disease progression)
  • Those same patient characteristics are also affected by prior treatment
  • The outcome of interest occurs at the end of a follow-up period during which both treatment and confounders evolve

The pattern is common in chronic disease epidemiology, pharmacoepidemiology, and comparative effectiveness research. A few examples:

  • Insulin dose adjustments based on HbA1c in diabetes
  • Immunosuppressant dosing based on kidney function in transplant recipients
  • Chemotherapy regimen changes based on tumor markers in oncology
  • Blood pressure medication titration based on BP readings

In every one of these settings, estimating causal effects correctly calls for the methods covered in this handbook rather than standard regression. The Causal Roadmap supplies the structure, and the estimation methods (g-computation, IPTW, TMLE, longitudinal TMLE) supply the tools.

Even after longitudinal methods, the results still rest on untestable identification assumptions. Chapter 16 showed how to probe that dependence with E-values, quantitative bias analysis, and truncation sensitivity. For the HAART cohort, unmeasured adherence is the most plausible threat: patients who adhere to treatment tend to be healthier, which can inflate the apparent benefit.
