26  Glossary of Abbreviations and Terms

This glossary collects abbreviations and short technical terms used throughout the handbook. Each entry gives a one- to two-sentence definition and, where useful, a pointer to the chapter that develops the concept in depth. It is intended as a quick reference, not as a substitute for the worked examples in the chapters themselves.

26.1 Causal framework and study design

ATE: Average Treatment Effect. The expected difference in potential outcomes if everyone in the target population were treated vs. if no one were treated: \(E[Y(1) - Y(0)]\). The default estimand for most chapters in this handbook. See Chapter 4.

ATT: Average Treatment Effect on the Treated. The expected difference in potential outcomes restricted to people who actually received treatment: \(E[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid A = 1]\).

Causal estimand. A summary of potential outcomes (e.g., the ATE) defined before any statistical procedure is chosen. Contrasted with a statistical estimand, which is a function of the observed-data distribution that equals the causal estimand under the identification assumptions. See Chapter 5, Step 3.

Causal gap. The difference between a causal estimand and the statistical estimand a procedure actually targets, which is nonzero whenever an identification assumption fails. Cannot be estimated directly; can be bounded by sensitivity analysis.

Causal Roadmap. The seven-step framework (question, structural causal model, estimand, identification, estimation, sensitivity analysis, interpretation) used to organize every analysis in this handbook. See Chapter 5.

Clean-room workflow. An analysis protocol in which the full statistical analysis plan (including estimator, nuisance learners, diagnostics, and stopping rules) is locked before the outcome data are seen, often with formal staging and external review. Reduces the credibility cost of investigator degrees of freedom in observational analyses.

DAG: Directed Acyclic Graph. A graph in which nodes are variables and directed arrows encode assumed causal relationships, with no directed cycles. Used to encode the structural causal model and to read off identification conditions. See Chapter 5, Step 2.

Estimand. The quantity we are trying to estimate, defined independently of the estimator used. See causal estimand and statistical estimand.

HTE: Heterogeneous Treatment Effects. Settings in which the treatment effect varies across subgroups defined by covariates; the object of estimation is usually the CATE.

ICH E9(R1). International Council for Harmonisation guideline on statistical principles for clinical trials, addendum on estimands and sensitivity analyses. Provides the regulatory framework for defining intercurrent-event strategies and aligning estimands with the scientific question. See Chapter 22.

ITT: Intention-to-Treat. Analysis strategy that classifies participants according to their assigned (or, in observational analogues, baseline) treatment, regardless of subsequent adherence or switching.

OR / RR / RD: Odds Ratio / Risk Ratio / Risk Difference. Three common scales for binary-outcome contrasts. The RD is the default in this handbook because it is collapsible and directly interpretable as an absolute risk change.

PP: Per-Protocol. Analysis strategy that estimates the effect of adhering to a treatment strategy, in contrast to ITT.

RCT: Randomized Controlled Trial. A study in which treatment is assigned by randomization, so baseline exchangeability holds by design.

RWD / RWE: Real-World Data / Real-World Evidence. Data collected outside controlled clinical trials (RWD) and the evidence derived from analyzing such data (RWE). The handbook treats RWE as observational target-trial emulation.

SCM: Structural Causal Model. A non-parametric system of structural equations representing the data-generating process. The DAG is a graphical projection of the SCM. See Chapter 5, Step 2.

SWIG: Single-World Intervention Graph. A graph that combines factual and counterfactual variables on a single diagram to read off identification under interventions. Used in some chapters but not the default presentation.

Target trial. A hypothetical randomized trial that the observational analysis is designed to emulate. The target trial protocol forces the analyst to specify eligibility, time zero, treatment strategies, assignment, outcome, follow-up, contrast, and analysis plan up front. See Chapter 5, Step 1.

Time zero / \(t_0\). The moment at which eligibility, treatment assignment, and the start of follow-up coincide in the target trial. Misalignment of time zero is the source of immortal-time bias.

26.2 Identification assumptions

Consistency. The potential outcome under treatment value \(a\) equals the observed outcome for individuals who actually received \(a\): \(Y(a) = Y\) when \(A = a\).

Exchangeability (no unmeasured confounding). Conditional on measured covariates \(W\), the potential outcomes are independent of the treatment assignment: \(Y(a) \perp A \mid W\).

Sequential exchangeability. The longitudinal extension of exchangeability: at each time point, the future potential outcomes are independent of the current treatment given covariate and treatment history.

Positivity / overlap. Every covariate stratum has nonzero probability of receiving each treatment value: \(0 < P(A = a \mid W) < 1\). Practical positivity violations occur when this probability is technically nonzero but extreme; structural violations occur when it is exactly zero by design or definition. See Chapter 11.

26.3 Estimation

AIPW: Augmented Inverse Probability Weighting. A doubly robust estimator that combines an outcome regression with an inverse-probability-of-treatment weighted residual term. Consistent if either the outcome model or the treatment model is correctly specified. See Chapter 8.

CATE: Conditional Average Treatment Effect. The treatment effect for individuals with a specified covariate profile: \(E[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid W = w]\). The object of estimation in the heterogeneity chapters. See Chapter 19.

DGP: Data-Generating Process. The (typically unknown) probabilistic mechanism that produced the observed data, often denoted \(P_0\). Simulation-based work defines the DGP explicitly; observational work treats it as an unknown to be estimated under structural assumptions.

G-computation. Estimation by fitting an outcome model, predicting each unit’s outcome under each treatment value, and averaging across the sample. The point-treatment instance of the g-formula. See Chapter 6.

G-formula / g-methods. The collection of identification formulas and estimation strategies (g-computation, IPTW with MSMs, g-estimation of structural nested models, TMLE) that handle time-varying treatments and confounders in the presence of treatment–confounder feedback.

HAL: Highly Adaptive Lasso. A non-parametric estimator with a convergence-rate guarantee fast enough to support efficient TMLE without sample-splitting. Used as a Super Learner candidate or as the nuisance estimator directly.

IOSW: Inverse Odds of Sampling Weights. Weights used in transportability and generalizability analyses to reweight a study sample so that it represents a target population. See Section 24.4.

IPCW: Inverse Probability of Censoring Weighting. Weights used to adjust for dependent (informative) censoring in time-to-event and longitudinal analyses.

IPTW: Inverse Probability of Treatment Weighting. Estimation by reweighting each individual by the inverse of their estimated probability of having received the treatment they actually got, so that the weighted sample mimics one in which treatment was randomized. See Chapter 7.

L-TMLE: Longitudinal TMLE. Extension of TMLE to longitudinal settings with multiple time points, time-varying treatments, and time-varying confounders. Implemented in the ltmle package. See Chapter 13.

LMTP: Longitudinal Modified Treatment Policies. A framework (and R package) for evaluating shift-style and dynamic-regime interventions in longitudinal data, particularly useful when static “always-treat” interventions violate positivity. See Chapter 2 and Part V.

MSM: Marginal Structural Model. A model for the marginal distribution of potential outcomes as a function of treatment strategy, typically fitted by IPTW. The original g-method for time-varying treatments.

Nuisance function. A component of the target parameter that is not itself of scientific interest but must be estimated en route, for example the propensity score and outcome regression in AIPW/TMLE.

Propensity score. \(P(A = 1 \mid W)\), the probability of treatment given covariates. The basis of IPTW and a key diagnostic for overlap.

SL: Super Learner. A cross-validated ensemble of candidate algorithms that selects the convex combination minimizing cross-validated risk. The default nuisance estimator throughout this handbook. See Chapter 9.

TMLE: Targeted Maximum Likelihood Estimation. A doubly robust, semiparametric efficient estimator that updates an initial outcome estimate using a targeting step driven by the propensity score, yielding asymptotically normal estimates and valid confidence intervals under weak conditions on the nuisance estimators. See Chapter 8.

26.4 Clinical / pharmacoepidemiology

ART / HAART. Antiretroviral therapy / Highly Active Antiretroviral Therapy. The combination antiretroviral regimens used in the running HIV case study; HAART is the historical term from the cohort era and is used throughout because the literature this dataset comes from uses it.

CD4. CD4+ T-cell count (cells/µL), the principal time-varying confounder in the HIV case study. Low CD4 is both a marker of disease severity (and therefore mortality risk) and a clinical trigger for initiating HAART.

Confounding by indication. The bias arising when the underlying disease severity that prompts treatment is also prognostic for the outcome, so that crude treated-vs-untreated comparisons reflect prognosis rather than the treatment effect.

Immortal time bias. The bias that arises when person-time before a treatment-defining event is misclassified as “exposed,” guaranteeing survival for the early portion of follow-up. Caught by correctly aligning time zero with treatment assignment. See Chapter 5.

Treatment–confounder feedback. The longitudinal pattern in which a time-varying covariate (e.g., CD4) is simultaneously a confounder of future treatment and a consequence of past treatment, so that no single regression can adjust for it without introducing bias. Motivates g-methods. See Chapter 12.
