Decomposing total effects into direct and indirect pathways

In development

This chapter is under active development. Content may be incomplete, change substantially, or contain placeholder material.

Estimated reading time: 60 minutes

If you only read one thing

Mediation splits a total effect into a part that runs through the mediator and a part that does not. In real data the defensible version uses interventional (and randomized-interventional) effects, with the recanting-twins decomposition when an intermediate confounder is itself affected by treatment. It is not the Baron-Kenny product of regression coefficients. The headline number is the indirect effect on the risk scale. The popular “proportion mediated” is fragile and should be read with caution.

22 Why This Matters

Readers who have worked through the LMTP adherence-shift methods chapter (Chapter 15) will recognize the idea of decomposing drug effects into adherence-mediated and pharmacological components. That decomposition was informal: it used distribution shifts to approximate a controlled direct effect. This chapter provides the formal mediation framework that places those intuitions on rigorous causal footing, with proper identification conditions and semiparametric efficient estimation (Laan and Rose 2011; Zheng and van der Laan 2011).

In pharmacoepidemiology, we often want to know not just whether a drug works, but how it works. Does highly active antiretroviral therapy (HAART) reduce mortality because it raises CD4 cell counts (indirect effect through the mediator), because of viral suppression at sites that act on mortality risk independently of CD4 (direct effect), or both?

Mediation analysis splits a total causal effect into the part that operates through a specified intermediate variable (the indirect effect) and the part that does not (the direct effect). Getting the split right requires careful attention to the estimand, the causal model, and the assumptions that connect the two. This chapter walks through the logic and implementation of TMLE-based mediation using the crumble R package.

What you will learn:

  • How to specify a mediation question as a formal causal estimand and why the choice of estimand matters.
  • The assumptions required for identification, and what breaks when they fail.
  • How TMLE-based estimation with cross-fitting and flexible learners works for mediation.
  • How to use the crumble package to estimate natural, randomized interventional, and recanting-twins effects.
  • How to interpret and report mediation results responsibly.
Sources and scope

This chapter is educational. Causal conclusions depend on identification assumptions (consistency, exchangeability, positivity) and on diagnostic evidence that the data support the target estimand. The worked examples use simulated data with known ground truth; real analyses require subject-matter justification for every assumption.

Key references: Zheng & van der Laan (2012), VanderWeele (2015), Díaz & Hejazi (2020), Robins & Richardson (2010), Williams & Díaz (2024, crumble). Full citations appear at the end.

23 Clinical Motivation

23.1 The Pharmacoepidemiologic Question

HAART has dramatically reduced mortality in HIV cohorts. A natural follow-up question:

How much of HAART’s effect on mortality operates through CD4 recovery (the immunologic pathway), and how much operates through other mechanisms (direct viral suppression, prevention of specific opportunistic infections, reduced systemic inflammation)?

The answer bears on whether immune reconstitution is a good treatment target, on monitoring strategies that lean on CD4 trajectories, and on how to read trials that use a surrogate endpoint (CD4 change) in place of mortality.

The Setup
  • Treatment (A): HAART initiation vs. deferred initiation
  • Mediator (M): 6-month CD4 change (immune reconstitution)
  • Outcome (Y): 1-year mortality (binary)
  • Baseline confounders (W): Age, BMI, baseline CD4, prior AIDS-defining illness, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), antiretroviral therapy (ART)-naive status
  • Question: How much of the \(A \to Y\) effect goes through \(A \to M \to Y\)?

  • A confounder is a common cause of treatment and outcome. Adjusting for it removes bias.
  • A moderator modifies the size of the treatment effect (interaction) but is not on the causal pathway.
  • A mediator is on the causal pathway from treatment to outcome. It is caused by treatment and in turn causes the outcome.
  • A collider is caused by two variables. Conditioning on a collider opens a spurious path.

Temporality is essential. A variable can only mediate the treatment effect if it occurs after treatment and before the outcome. Treating a post-outcome variable, or one measured cross-sectionally, as a mediator is a common error, and it can introduce bias rather than remove it.

The intermediate confounder problem

A variable \(Z\) that is affected by treatment and confounds the mediator-outcome relationship is the hardest problem in mediation analysis. If \(Z\) exists and you ignore it, your decomposition is biased. We return to this below when choosing the estimand.

25 Mediation Analysis: The Causal Roadmap

25.1 Step 1: Define the Estimand

Mediation splits a total effect into pieces. There are several competing definitions, and the choice matters because they require different assumptions.

Natural direct and indirect effects (NDE/NIE). These are the most common definitions (Robins and Greenland 1992; Pearl 2001). In plain language, the NDE captures the part of the treatment effect that bypasses the mediator entirely, while the NIE captures the part that works through the mediator. Together they add up to the total effect.

\[\text{NDE} = E[Y(1, M(0))] - E[Y(0, M(0))]\] \[\text{NIE} = E[Y(1, M(1))] - E[Y(1, M(0))]\]

The NDE asks: what is the effect of treatment if we could hold the mediator at the value it would have taken under control? The NIE asks: what is the effect of shifting the mediator as treatment would, while keeping treatment on? These decompose the total effect: \(\text{TE} = \text{NDE} + \text{NIE}\).

The trouble is that \(Y(1, M(0))\) is a cross-world quantity that combines treatment from one “world” (\(A=1\)) with a mediator value from another (\(A=0\)). Pinning it down requires a strong, untestable assumption: no treatment-induced mediator-outcome confounding (Zheng and van der Laan 2011; Hernán and Robins 2025).

The natural indirect effect answers: how much of the total effect operates through the mediator? In the HAART example, the NIE quantifies how much of the mortality benefit is attributable to CD4 recovery specifically.

Randomized interventional effects. Instead of setting \(M\) to a specific counterfactual value, we draw \(M\) from its distribution under control (Tyler J. VanderWeele, Vansteelandt, and Robins 2014). Think of this as asking: “What would the outcome look like if we kept the treatment on, but randomly reassigned each patient’s mediator value from the pool of mediator values seen under the control condition?” This avoids the cross-world problem by using distributions rather than fixed individual values. The two components are the interventional direct effect (IDE) and the interventional indirect effect (IIE):

\[\text{IDE} = E[Y(1, G_0)] - E[Y(0, G_0)]\] \[\text{IIE} = E[Y(1, G_1)] - E[Y(1, G_0)]\]

where \(G_a\) denotes a random draw from \(P(M \mid A = a, W)\). These do not require cross-world quantities and are identified under weaker conditions.

Recanting twins (RT). When there is an intermediate confounder \(Z\) (affected by treatment, confounding the mediator-outcome path), even randomized interventional effects need further care. The recanting-twins approach (Robins & Richardson, 2010) splits the effect into path-specific components using two independent “copies” of the treatment.

Which estimand should you choose?
  • If you are confident there is no intermediate confounder (no \(Z\)), natural effects ("N") are the most interpretable and standard choice.
  • If intermediate confounding is plausible or you want weaker assumptions, randomized interventional effects ("RI") are a good default.
  • If you have a measured intermediate confounder \(Z\) and want to decompose the effect further, recanting twins ("RT") handles this explicitly.

For this tutorial, we will estimate all three to compare.

25.3 Step 3: Identification Assumptions

To identify mediation effects, we need the standard causal assumptions from Chapter 5 (consistency, exchangeability, positivity) plus additional ones specific to the mediator pathway:

  1. Consistency: as in Chapter 5.

  2. No unmeasured treatment-outcome confounding: \(Y(a, m) \perp\!\!\!\perp A \mid W\). In the HAART example, this means that, conditional on baseline covariates, treatment initiation is unrelated to potential mortality outcomes. This is the standard assumption from any observational ATE analysis.

  3. No unmeasured treatment-mediator confounding: \(M(a) \perp\!\!\!\perp A \mid W\). The mediator (CD4 change) must be independent of treatment assignment given \(W\). If physicians initiate HAART preferentially in patients they expect will mount a good immune response, and that expectation is based on unmeasured factors, this assumption fails.

  4. No unmeasured mediator-outcome confounding: \(Y(a, m) \perp\!\!\!\perp M \mid A, W\) (or \(A, W, Z\) if intermediate confounders exist). This fails, for example, if unmeasured adherence patterns independently affect both CD4 change and mortality.

  5. Positivity: For all strata of \(W\), both treatment values have positive probability, and all mediator values observed under one treatment could also occur under the other. If HAART always produces CD4 increases larger than any observed under deferred initiation, the density ratios needed for estimation become extreme.

For natural effects only, add:

  1. No treatment-induced mediator-outcome confounding: No confounder of \(M \to Y\) is affected by \(A\). This is the assumption that fails when an intermediate confounder \(Z\) exists. It fails, for example, if HAART changes a patient’s weight trajectory, which independently affects both CD4 dynamics and mortality risk. Together with assumptions 2 to 4, this condition delivers the cross-world independence that natural effects require; the genuinely untestable cross-world statement is \(Y(1, m) \perp\!\!\!\perp M(0) \mid W\).

For randomized interventional and recanting twins effects, assumption 6 is replaced by the weaker requirement that intermediate confounders are measured and adjusted for.

25.4 Step 4: Estimation Strategy

TMLE-based mediation estimation (as implemented in crumble) works in several stages. Throughout, the target stays the same: split the total treatment effect into the portion that operates through the mediator (the indirect effect) and the portion that does not (the direct effect).

  1. Nuisance estimation with cross-fitting. Fit flexible models for the outcome (\(E[Y \mid A, M, W]\)), the mediator distribution (\(P(M \mid A, W)\)), and the treatment mechanism (\(P(A \mid W)\)) using an ensemble of learners. Cross-fitting (sample splitting) avoids overfitting bias.

  2. Riesz representer estimation. The targeting step in TMLE requires a “clever covariate” that depends on the estimand. For mediation, this involves density ratios of the mediator distribution under treatment vs. control. The crumble package estimates these with a neural network (the nn_module argument) rather than requiring explicit density estimation.

  3. Targeting (fluctuation). Update the initial outcome model to solve the efficient influence function equation. This makes the estimator multiply robust: it stays consistent if any two of the three nuisance components (the outcome model, the mediator density, and the treatment propensity) are correctly specified, under appropriate regularity conditions (Tchetgen Tchetgen and Shpitser 2012).

  4. Plug-in estimation and inference. Compute the effect estimate from the targeted predictions and obtain standard errors from the influence function.

Technical detail: the mediation g-computation formulas (expand)

Under the identification assumptions, the NDE and NIE can be written as weighted averages over the outcome model and the mediator distribution. The NDE formula compares the expected outcome under treatment versus control, holding the mediator distribution at its control-arm values. The NIE formula holds treatment on and asks how the outcome changes when the mediator distribution shifts from its control-arm shape to its treatment-arm shape.

\[\text{NDE} = \sum_m \sum_w \big[E[Y \mid A=1, M=m, W=w] - E[Y \mid A=0, M=m, W=w]\big] \cdot P(M=m \mid A=0, W=w) \cdot P(W=w)\]

\[\text{NIE} = \sum_m \sum_w E[Y \mid A=1, M=m, W=w] \cdot \big[P(M=m \mid A=1, W=w) - P(M=m \mid A=0, W=w)\big] \cdot P(W=w)\]

These require an outcome model \(E[Y \mid A, M, W]\), a mediator model \(P(M \mid A, W)\), and marginalization over \(W\). TMLE targets these functionals to achieve double robustness.

25.5 Step 5: Diagnostics, Sensitivity, and Reporting

After estimation, check:

  • Propensity score overlap: extreme treatment probabilities signal positivity problems.
  • Mediator distribution overlap: if the mediator distributions under treatment and control barely overlap, the density ratios will be extreme and estimates unstable.
  • Influence function diagnostics: large or highly variable influence function values indicate fragile estimates.
  • Sensitivity analysis: consider how much unmeasured confounding would be needed to explain away the result (e.g., E-values for mediation, or simulation-based sensitivity).

26.1 True Causal Effects

We compute ground-truth effects from the structural model via Monte Carlo integration.

Show code 
set.seed(999)
n_mc <- 50

# Mediator and Z means under each treatment
Z_mean_A0 <- 1.5 * 0 + 0.05 * (bmi - 24) - 0.02 * (age - 38)
Z_mean_A1 <- 1.5 * 1 + 0.05 * (bmi - 24) - 0.02 * (age - 38)

compute_EY <- function(a_val, m_vals, z_vals, idx) {
  lp <- -3.5 +
    -0.25 * a_val +
    -0.004 * m_vals +
    -0.05 * z_vals +
     0.03 * (age[idx] - 38) +
     0.02 * (bmi[idx] - 24) +
     0.90 * prior_aids[idx] +
    -0.01 * (egfr[idx] - 80) +
     0.25 * art_naive[idx] +
     0.15 * a_val * prior_aids[idx]
  mean(plogis(lp))
}

true_nde_vals <- numeric(n)
true_nie_vals <- numeric(n)

for (i in 1:n) {
  # Draw Z from P(Z | A=0, W) and P(Z | A=1, W)
  z0 <- rnorm(n_mc, mean = Z_mean_A0[i], sd = 2)
  z1 <- rnorm(n_mc, mean = Z_mean_A1[i], sd = 2)

  # M means given Z draws
  M_mean_fn <- function(a, z) {
    20 + 80 * a - 0.20 * (cd4_bl[i] - 280) +
      0.5 * egfr[i] - 0.5 * (age[i] - 38) + 4 * z
  }

  # Draw M from P(M | A=0, Z(0), W)
  m_A0_Z0 <- rnorm(n_mc, mean = M_mean_fn(0, z0), sd = 35)
  # Draw M from P(M | A=1, Z(1), W)
  m_A1_Z1 <- rnorm(n_mc, mean = M_mean_fn(1, z1), sd = 35)

  # NDE: E[Y(1, M(0), Z(0))] - E[Y(0, M(0), Z(0))]
  # (holding mediator and Z at control values)
  EY_1_M0 <- compute_EY(1, m_A0_Z0, z0, i)
  EY_0_M0 <- compute_EY(0, m_A0_Z0, z0, i)
  EY_1_M1 <- compute_EY(1, m_A1_Z1, z1, i)

  true_nde_vals[i] <- EY_1_M0 - EY_0_M0
  # This "indirect" term shifts BOTH the mediator M and the treatment-induced
  # confounder Z from their control to their treatment distributions, so it
  # bundles A -> M -> Y with the A -> Z -> Y and A -> Z -> M -> Y pathways. It is
  # not a mediator-only (natural) indirect effect, which is not identified here.
  true_nie_vals[i] <- EY_1_M1 - EY_1_M0
}

true_NDE <- mean(true_nde_vals)
true_NIE <- mean(true_nie_vals)
true_TE  <- true_NDE + true_NIE

cat("True Total Effect:", round(true_TE, 4), "\n")
#> True Total Effect: -0.0123
cat("True direct effect (NDE, M and Z at control):   ", round(true_NDE, 4), "\n")
#> True direct effect (NDE, M and Z at control):    -0.0053
cat("True indirect effect (through M and A-induced Z):", round(true_NIE, 4), "\n")
#> True indirect effect (through M and A-induced Z): -0.007
cat("Direct + indirect:                              ", round(true_NDE + true_NIE, 4), "\n")
#> Direct + indirect:                               -0.0123

Because \(Z\) is affected by treatment and confounds \(M \to Y\), the effect through \(M\) alone is not identified as a natural indirect effect (it would require the cross-world independence that a treatment-induced confounder violates). The “indirect” quantity above is a valid split of the total effect, but it moves \(M\) together with \(Z\), so it also carries the \(A \to Z \to Y\) and \(A \to Z \to M \to Y\) pathways. Separating the mediator-specific contribution requires the randomized interventional direct and indirect effects that crumble targets, where \(Z\) is drawn from a fixed reference distribution rather than allowed to react to the shifted treatment.

27 Why Naive Approaches Fail

27.1 The Baron-Kenny Trap

The classic Baron-Kenny approach fits regressions for \(Y \sim A\) and \(Y \sim A + M\), then reads off coefficients. For binary outcomes, this fails for several reasons:

Show code 
total_mod <- glm(Y ~ A + age + bmi + cd4_bl + prior_aids + egfr + art_naive,
                 family = binomial, data = dat)

full_mod  <- glm(Y ~ A + M + age + bmi + cd4_bl + prior_aids + egfr + art_naive,
                 family = binomial, data = dat)

med_mod   <- lm(M ~ A + age + bmi + cd4_bl + prior_aids + egfr + art_naive,
                data = dat)

cat("Total effect (A without M):", round(coef(total_mod)["A"], 3), "(log-OR)\n")
#> Total effect (A without M): -0.894 (log-OR)
cat("Direct effect (A with M):  ", round(coef(full_mod)["A"], 3), "(log-OR)\n")
#> Direct effect (A with M):   -0.091 (log-OR)
cat("Treatment on mediator:     ", round(coef(med_mod)["A"], 3), "(mean change)\n")
#> Treatment on mediator:      89.941 (mean change)

Why this is wrong:

  1. Non-collapsibility. The log-odds ratio changes when you add \(M\) even if there is no mediation, simply because the logistic link is non-collapsible. The “change in coefficient” is not the indirect effect.

  2. Scale dependence. The difference-in-coefficients method requires additivity on the scale of the link function, which logistic regression does not provide for risk differences.

  3. Post-treatment confounding. Adding \(M\) (a post-treatment variable) to the regression without proper identification opens backdoor paths through any \(Z\) that affects both \(M\) and \(Y\).

  4. No valid inference. Standard errors for the “indirect effect” (product of coefficients) rely on normality assumptions that are wrong for binary outcomes.

These are not minor quibbles. They produce qualitatively misleading answers. A proper mediation analysis specifies the estimand, verifies identification, and uses an estimator matched to both.

29 Interpretation

29.1 What do the effects mean in context?

In the HAART example:

  • The direct effect captures the mortality benefit of HAART that does not operate through CD4 recovery: viral suppression acting on tissues other than CD4 lymphocytes, prevention of opportunistic infections through reduced viral burden, and lower systemic inflammation independent of CD4 count. A large direct effect suggests that monitoring HAART by CD4 alone misses an important part of the drug’s protective action.

  • The indirect effect (via M) captures how much of the mortality benefit is attributable to immune reconstitution as measured by CD4 change. If this is large, CD4 trajectory is a strong surrogate for the mortality benefit; if small, CD4 monitoring is not capturing the dominant mechanism.

  • The total effect is the sum (for additive decompositions). It should be close to what you would estimate from a standard TMLE or IPTW analysis of \(A\) on \(Y\).

The choice of effect definition matters for interpretation. Natural effects answer “what if we could intervene on the mediator to hold it at its control value?” This is a hypothetical intervention that may not be physically realizable.

Randomized interventional effects answer a different question: “what if we randomly assigned the mediator value from its distribution under control?” This stochastic intervention is closer to policy-relevant questions. Recanting twins decompose the total effect into path-specific components that remain identifiable even with intermediate confounding.

None of these claims establish “mechanism” in a biological sense without strong assumptions. The decomposition tells you how much of the effect flows through the measured mediator given the causal model and identification assumptions. Unmeasured mediator-outcome confounders, measurement error in \(M\), or a misspecified time ordering can all distort it.

29.2 Caution: proportion mediated

The “proportion mediated” (NIE / TE) is commonly reported but has several problems (Tyler J. VanderWeele and Knol 2014):

  • It is undefined when the total effect is zero or near zero.
  • It can be greater than 1 or negative when the direct and indirect effects have opposite signs.
  • It is highly variable because it is a ratio of two estimated quantities, each with uncertainty.
  • It is scale-dependent: the proportion mediated on the risk-difference scale differs from that on the odds-ratio scale.

Report the direct and indirect effects on the absolute scale (risk difference) with confidence intervals. If you report proportion mediated, note these caveats.

31 What to Report

When publishing a mediation analysis, include:

  1. Effect definition: Which estimand (natural, interventional, recanting twins) and why.
  2. Time ordering: When each variable was measured and why the ordering supports mediation claims.
  3. Confounder sets: Which variables are treated as baseline confounders (\(W\)), mediators (\(M\)), and intermediate confounders (\(Z\)), with justification.
  4. Identification assumptions: State them explicitly. Note which are testable and which require subject-matter judgment.
  5. Estimator details: TMLE with cross-fitting, number of folds, learner library.
  6. Point estimates and uncertainty: Effect estimates on the risk-difference scale with 95% confidence intervals.
  7. Diagnostics: Propensity score overlap, mediator distribution overlap, density ratio stability.
  8. Sensitivity analysis: How robust are conclusions to unmeasured confounding or model misspecification?
  9. Limitations: Measurement error in the mediator, unmeasured pathways, temporal resolution of the data.

32 Key Takeaways

Summary

  1. Mediation analysis decomposes a total effect into direct and indirect components. This tells us how much of a drug’s benefit flows through a specific mechanism, but only under strong assumptions.

  2. The choice of estimand matters. Natural effects require the cross-world assumption (no treatment-induced mediator-outcome confounders). Randomized interventional effects and recanting twins relax this at the cost of a slightly different interpretation.

  3. Baron-Kenny regression fails for binary outcomes because of non-collapsibility and post-treatment confounding. Even for continuous outcomes, it assumes linearity and no interactions.

  4. TMLE-based mediation (via crumble) combines flexible nuisance estimation, cross-fitting, and targeting to provide multiply robust estimates (consistent if any two of the outcome model, mediator density, and treatment propensity are correct) with valid inference under appropriate regularity conditions.

  5. Diagnostics are essential: propensity score overlap, mediator distribution shift, density ratio stability, and the mediator-outcome relationship should all be examined.

  6. Report responsibly. State the estimand, assumptions, time ordering, and limitations. Avoid over-interpreting “proportion mediated” or claiming biological mechanism without acknowledging the assumptions required.

# Why This Matters

Readers who have worked through the LMTP adherence-shift methods chapter (@sec-adherence-shift) will recognize the idea of decomposing drug effects into adherence-mediated and pharmacological components. That decomposition was *informal*: it used distribution shifts to approximate a controlled direct effect. This chapter provides the formal mediation framework that places those intuitions on rigorous causal footing, with proper identification conditions and semiparametric efficient estimation [@vanderLaan2011targeted; @zheng2011naturaldirect].

In pharmacoepidemiology, we often want to know not just whether a drug works, but how it works. Does highly active antiretroviral therapy (HAART) reduce mortality because it raises CD4 cell counts (indirect effect through the mediator), because of viral suppression at sites that act on mortality risk independently of CD4 (direct effect), or both?

Mediation analysis splits a total causal effect into the part that operates through a specified intermediate variable (the indirect effect) and the part that does not (the direct effect). Getting the split right requires careful attention to the estimand, the causal model, and the assumptions that connect the two. This chapter walks through the logic and implementation of TMLE-based mediation using the `crumble` R package.

**What you will learn:**

- How to specify a mediation question as a formal causal estimand and why the choice of estimand matters.
- The assumptions required for identification, and what breaks when they fail.
- How TMLE-based estimation with cross-fitting and flexible learners works for mediation.
- How to use the `crumble` package to estimate natural, randomized interventional, and recanting-twins effects.
- How to interpret and report mediation results responsibly.

# Clinical Motivation

## The Pharmacoepidemiologic Question

HAART has dramatically reduced mortality in HIV cohorts. A natural follow-up question:

> **How much of HAART's effect on mortality operates through CD4 recovery (the immunologic pathway), and how much operates through other mechanisms (direct viral suppression, prevention of specific opportunistic infections, reduced systemic inflammation)?**

The answer bears on whether immune reconstitution is a good treatment target, on monitoring strategies that lean on CD4 trajectories, and on how to read trials that use a surrogate endpoint (CD4 change) in place of mortality.

::: {.callout-note}
## The Setup

- **Treatment (A):** HAART initiation vs. deferred initiation
- **Mediator (M):** 6-month CD4 change (immune reconstitution)
- **Outcome (Y):** 1-year mortality (binary)
- **Baseline confounders (W):** Age, BMI, baseline CD4, prior AIDS-defining illness, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), antiretroviral therapy (ART)-naive status
- **Question:** How much of the $A \to Y$ effect goes through $A \to M \to Y$?
:::

- A **confounder** is a common cause of treatment and outcome. Adjusting for it removes bias.
- A **moderator** modifies the size of the treatment effect (interaction) but is not on the causal pathway.
- A **mediator** is on the causal pathway from treatment to outcome. It is caused by treatment and in turn causes the outcome.
- A **collider** is caused by two variables. Conditioning on a collider opens a spurious path.

**Temporality is essential.** A variable can only mediate the treatment effect if it occurs *after* treatment and *before* the outcome. Treating a post-outcome variable, or one measured cross-sectionally, as a mediator is a common error, and it can introduce bias rather than remove it.

# Mediation Analysis: The Causal Roadmap

## Step 1: Define the Estimand

Mediation splits a total effect into pieces. There are several competing definitions, and the choice matters because they require different assumptions.

**Natural direct and indirect effects (NDE/NIE).** These are the most common definitions [@robinsgreenland1992; @pearl2001mediation]. In plain language, the NDE captures the part of the treatment effect that bypasses the mediator entirely, while the NIE captures the part that works through the mediator. Together they add up to the total effect.

$$\text{NDE} = E[Y(1, M(0))] - E[Y(0, M(0))]$$
$$\text{NIE} = E[Y(1, M(1))] - E[Y(1, M(0))]$$

The NDE asks: what is the effect of treatment if we could hold the mediator at the value it would have taken under control? The NIE asks: what is the effect of shifting the mediator as treatment would, while keeping treatment on? These decompose the total effect: $\text{TE} = \text{NDE} + \text{NIE}$.

The trouble is that $Y(1, M(0))$ is a **cross-world** quantity that combines treatment from one "world" ($A=1$) with a mediator value from another ($A=0$). Pinning it down requires a strong, untestable assumption: no treatment-induced mediator-outcome confounding [@zheng2011naturaldirect; @hernan2025whatif].

The natural indirect effect answers: how much of the total effect operates through the mediator? In the HAART example, the NIE quantifies how much of the mortality benefit is attributable to CD4 recovery specifically.

**Randomized interventional effects.** Instead of setting $M$ to a specific counterfactual value, we draw $M$ from its distribution under control [@vanderweele2014mediation]. Think of this as asking: "What would the outcome look like if we kept the treatment on, but randomly reassigned each patient's mediator value from the pool of mediator values seen under the control condition?" This avoids the cross-world problem by using distributions rather than fixed individual values. The two components are the **interventional direct effect (IDE)** and the **interventional indirect effect (IIE)**:

$$\text{IDE} = E[Y(1, G_0)] - E[Y(0, G_0)]$$
$$\text{IIE} = E[Y(1, G_1)] - E[Y(1, G_0)]$$

where $G_a$ denotes a random draw from $P(M \mid A = a, W)$. These do not require cross-world quantities and are identified under weaker conditions.

**Recanting twins (RT).** When there is an intermediate confounder $Z$ (affected by treatment, confounding the mediator-outcome path), even randomized interventional effects need further care. The recanting-twins approach (Robins & Richardson, 2010) splits the effect into path-specific components using two independent "copies" of the treatment.

::: {.callout-tip}
## Which estimand should you choose?

- If you are confident there is **no intermediate confounder** (no $Z$), natural effects (`"N"`) are the most interpretable and standard choice.
- If intermediate confounding is plausible or you want **weaker assumptions**, randomized interventional effects (`"RI"`) are a good default.
- If you have a **measured intermediate confounder** $Z$ and want to decompose the effect further, recanting twins (`"RT"`) handles this explicitly.

For this tutorial, we will estimate all three to compare.
:::

## Step 3: Identification Assumptions

To identify mediation effects, we need the standard causal assumptions from @sec-roadmap (consistency, exchangeability, positivity) **plus** additional ones specific to the mediator pathway:

1. **Consistency:** as in @sec-roadmap.

2. **No unmeasured treatment-outcome confounding:** $Y(a, m) \perp\!\!\!\perp A \mid W$. In the HAART example, this means that, conditional on baseline covariates, treatment initiation is unrelated to potential mortality outcomes. This is the standard assumption from any observational ATE analysis.

3. **No unmeasured treatment-mediator confounding:** $M(a) \perp\!\!\!\perp A \mid W$. The mediator (CD4 change) must be independent of treatment assignment given $W$. If physicians initiate HAART preferentially in patients they expect will mount a good immune response, and that expectation is based on unmeasured factors, this assumption fails.

4. **No unmeasured mediator-outcome confounding:** $Y(a, m) \perp\!\!\!\perp M \mid A, W$ (or $A, W, Z$ if intermediate confounders exist). This fails, for example, if unmeasured adherence patterns independently affect both CD4 change and mortality.

5. **Positivity:** For all strata of $W$, both treatment values have positive probability, and all mediator values observed under one treatment could also occur under the other. If HAART always produces CD4 increases larger than any observed under deferred initiation, the density ratios needed for estimation become extreme.

For **natural effects** only, add:

6. **No treatment-induced mediator-outcome confounding:** No confounder of $M \to Y$ is affected by $A$. This is the assumption that fails when an intermediate confounder $Z$ exists. It fails, for example, if HAART changes a patient's weight trajectory, which independently affects both CD4 dynamics and mortality risk. Together with assumptions 2 to 4, this condition delivers the cross-world independence that natural effects require; the genuinely untestable cross-world statement is $Y(1, m) \perp\!\!\!\perp M(0) \mid W$.

For randomized interventional and recanting twins effects, assumption 6 is replaced by the weaker requirement that intermediate confounders are measured and adjusted for.

::: {.callout-note collapse="true"}
## Assumption checklist (expand)

Before running a mediation analysis, verify:

- [ ] Treatment precedes mediator precedes outcome in time.
- [ ] Baseline confounders $W$ are measured before treatment.
- [ ] No variable in $W$ is itself affected by treatment (otherwise it belongs in $Z$ or $M$).
- [ ] Confounders of $A \to Y$, $A \to M$, and $M \to Y$ are identified and measured.
- [ ] If using natural effects: no treatment-induced mediator-outcome confounder exists (or if one does, switch to RI/RT).
- [ ] If using RI or RT effects: intermediate confounders are measured and specified in the model.
- [ ] Positivity: treatment groups overlap in confounder space; mediator distributions overlap across treatment arms.
- [ ] The mediator is not a proxy for treatment (if $M$ is nearly deterministic given $A$, the indirect effect is poorly identified).

None of these can be fully verified from data. Subject-matter knowledge is required.
:::


## Step 4: Estimation Strategy

TMLE-based mediation estimation (as implemented in `crumble`) works in several stages. Throughout, the target stays the same: split the total treatment effect into the portion that operates through the mediator (the indirect effect) and the portion that does not (the direct effect).

1. **Nuisance estimation with cross-fitting.** Fit flexible models for the outcome ($E[Y \mid A, M, W]$), the mediator distribution ($P(M \mid A, W)$), and the treatment mechanism ($P(A \mid W)$) using an ensemble of learners. Cross-fitting (sample splitting) avoids overfitting bias.

2. **Riesz representer estimation.** The targeting step in TMLE requires a "clever covariate" that depends on the estimand. For mediation, this involves density ratios of the mediator distribution under treatment vs. control. The `crumble` package estimates these with a neural network (the `nn_module` argument) rather than requiring explicit density estimation.

3. **Targeting (fluctuation).** Update the initial outcome model to solve the efficient influence function equation. This makes the estimator multiply robust: it stays consistent if any two of the three nuisance components (the outcome model, the mediator density, and the treatment propensity) are correctly specified, under appropriate regularity conditions [@tchetgen2012mediation].

4. **Plug-in estimation and inference.** Compute the effect estimate from the targeted predictions and obtain standard errors from the influence function.

::: {.callout-note collapse="true"}
## Technical detail: the mediation g-computation formulas (expand)

Under the identification assumptions, the NDE and NIE can be written as weighted averages over the outcome model and the mediator distribution. The NDE formula compares the expected outcome under treatment versus control, holding the mediator distribution at its control-arm values. The NIE formula holds treatment on and asks how the outcome changes when the mediator distribution shifts from its control-arm shape to its treatment-arm shape.

$$\text{NDE} = \sum_m \sum_w \big[E[Y \mid A=1, M=m, W=w] - E[Y \mid A=0, M=m, W=w]\big] \cdot P(M=m \mid A=0, W=w) \cdot P(W=w)$$

$$\text{NIE} = \sum_m \sum_w E[Y \mid A=1, M=m, W=w] \cdot \big[P(M=m \mid A=1, W=w) - P(M=m \mid A=0, W=w)\big] \cdot P(W=w)$$

These require an outcome model $E[Y \mid A, M, W]$, a mediator model $P(M \mid A, W)$, and marginalization over $W$. TMLE targets these functionals to achieve double robustness.
:::


## Step 5: Diagnostics, Sensitivity, and Reporting

After estimation, check:

- **Propensity score overlap:** extreme treatment probabilities signal positivity problems.
- **Mediator distribution overlap:** if the mediator distributions under treatment and control barely overlap, the density ratios will be extreme and estimates unstable.
- **Influence function diagnostics:** large or highly variable influence function values indicate fragile estimates.
- **Sensitivity analysis:** consider how much unmeasured confounding would be needed to explain away the result (e.g., E-values for mediation, or simulation-based sensitivity).

We demonstrate diagnostics in the worked example below.

## True Causal Effects

We compute ground-truth effects from the structural model via Monte Carlo integration.

```{r}
#| cache: true
set.seed(999)
n_mc <- 50

# Mediator and Z means under each treatment
Z_mean_A0 <- 1.5 * 0 + 0.05 * (bmi - 24) - 0.02 * (age - 38)
Z_mean_A1 <- 1.5 * 1 + 0.05 * (bmi - 24) - 0.02 * (age - 38)

compute_EY <- function(a_val, m_vals, z_vals, idx) {
  lp <- -3.5 +
    -0.25 * a_val +
    -0.004 * m_vals +
    -0.05 * z_vals +
     0.03 * (age[idx] - 38) +
     0.02 * (bmi[idx] - 24) +
     0.90 * prior_aids[idx] +
    -0.01 * (egfr[idx] - 80) +
     0.25 * art_naive[idx] +
     0.15 * a_val * prior_aids[idx]
  mean(plogis(lp))
}

true_nde_vals <- numeric(n)
true_nie_vals <- numeric(n)

for (i in 1:n) {
  # Draw Z from P(Z | A=0, W) and P(Z | A=1, W)
  z0 <- rnorm(n_mc, mean = Z_mean_A0[i], sd = 2)
  z1 <- rnorm(n_mc, mean = Z_mean_A1[i], sd = 2)

  # M means given Z draws
  M_mean_fn <- function(a, z) {
    20 + 80 * a - 0.20 * (cd4_bl[i] - 280) +
      0.5 * egfr[i] - 0.5 * (age[i] - 38) + 4 * z
  }

  # Draw M from P(M | A=0, Z(0), W)
  m_A0_Z0 <- rnorm(n_mc, mean = M_mean_fn(0, z0), sd = 35)
  # Draw M from P(M | A=1, Z(1), W)
  m_A1_Z1 <- rnorm(n_mc, mean = M_mean_fn(1, z1), sd = 35)

  # NDE: E[Y(1, M(0), Z(0))] - E[Y(0, M(0), Z(0))]
  # (holding mediator and Z at control values)
  EY_1_M0 <- compute_EY(1, m_A0_Z0, z0, i)
  EY_0_M0 <- compute_EY(0, m_A0_Z0, z0, i)
  EY_1_M1 <- compute_EY(1, m_A1_Z1, z1, i)

  true_nde_vals[i] <- EY_1_M0 - EY_0_M0
  # This "indirect" term shifts BOTH the mediator M and the treatment-induced
  # confounder Z from their control to their treatment distributions, so it
  # bundles A -> M -> Y with the A -> Z -> Y and A -> Z -> M -> Y pathways. It is
  # not a mediator-only (natural) indirect effect, which is not identified here.
  true_nie_vals[i] <- EY_1_M1 - EY_1_M0
}

true_NDE <- mean(true_nde_vals)
true_NIE <- mean(true_nie_vals)
true_TE  <- true_NDE + true_NIE

cat("True Total Effect:", round(true_TE, 4), "\n")
cat("True direct effect (NDE, M and Z at control):   ", round(true_NDE, 4), "\n")
cat("True indirect effect (through M and A-induced Z):", round(true_NIE, 4), "\n")
cat("Direct + indirect:                              ", round(true_NDE + true_NIE, 4), "\n")
```

Because $Z$ is affected by treatment and confounds $M \to Y$, the effect through $M$ *alone* is not identified as a natural indirect effect (it would require the cross-world independence that a treatment-induced confounder violates). The "indirect" quantity above is a valid split of the total effect, but it moves $M$ together with $Z$, so it also carries the $A \to Z \to Y$ and $A \to Z \to M \to Y$ pathways. Separating the mediator-specific contribution requires the *randomized interventional* direct and indirect effects that `crumble` targets, where $Z$ is drawn from a fixed reference distribution rather than allowed to react to the shifted treatment.


# Why Naive Approaches Fail

## The Baron-Kenny Trap

The classic Baron-Kenny approach fits regressions for $Y \sim A$ and $Y \sim A + M$, then reads off coefficients. For binary outcomes, this fails for several reasons:

```{r}
total_mod <- glm(Y ~ A + age + bmi + cd4_bl + prior_aids + egfr + art_naive,
                 family = binomial, data = dat)

full_mod  <- glm(Y ~ A + M + age + bmi + cd4_bl + prior_aids + egfr + art_naive,
                 family = binomial, data = dat)

med_mod   <- lm(M ~ A + age + bmi + cd4_bl + prior_aids + egfr + art_naive,
                data = dat)

cat("Total effect (A without M):", round(coef(total_mod)["A"], 3), "(log-OR)\n")
cat("Direct effect (A with M):  ", round(coef(full_mod)["A"], 3), "(log-OR)\n")
cat("Treatment on mediator:     ", round(coef(med_mod)["A"], 3), "(mean change)\n")
```

**Why this is wrong:**

1. **Non-collapsibility.** The log-odds ratio changes when you add $M$ even if there is no mediation, simply because the logistic link is non-collapsible. The "change in coefficient" is not the indirect effect.

2. **Scale dependence.** The difference-in-coefficients method requires additivity on the scale of the link function, which logistic regression does not provide for risk differences.

3. **Post-treatment confounding.** Adding $M$ (a post-treatment variable) to the regression without proper identification opens backdoor paths through any $Z$ that affects both $M$ and $Y$.

4. **No valid inference.** Standard errors for the "indirect effect" (product of coefficients) rely on normality assumptions that are wrong for binary outcomes.

These are not minor quibbles. They produce qualitatively misleading answers. A proper mediation analysis specifies the estimand, verifies identification, and uses an estimator matched to both.


# Worked Example: Mediation with `crumble`

The `crumble` package (Williams & Díaz, 2024) implements TMLE-based mediation analysis with cross-fitting, flexible machine-learning nuisance estimation, and neural-network Riesz representer estimation. It supports natural effects, randomized interventional effects, organic effects, and recanting twins.

## Example 1: `weight_behavior` Dataset

We start with the example dataset from `mma::weight_behavior`, which is used in the `crumble` README and is known to work.

```{r}
#| eval: false
#| code-fold: true
library(crumble)
data(weight_behavior, package = "mma")

# Treatment: sports participation (factor with levels 1, 2)
# Outcome: bmi (continuous)
# Mediators: exercises, overweigh
# Intermediate confounder: snack (affected by sports, confounds mediator→outcome)
# Covariates: age, sex, tvhours

# --- Natural effects (assumes no intermediate confounding) ---
fit_natural <- crumble(
  data      = weight_behavior,
  trt       = "sports",
  outcome   = "bmi",
  covar     = c("age", "sex", "tvhours"),          # <1>
  mediators = c("exercises", "overweigh"),           # <2>
  d0 = \(data, trt) factor(rep(1, nrow(data)), levels = c("1", "2")),  # <3>
  d1 = \(data, trt) factor(rep(2, nrow(data)), levels = c("1", "2")),  # <3>
  effect    = "N",                                   # <4>
  learners  = c("mean", "glm", "earth", "ranger"),   # <5>
  nn_module = sequential_module(),                   # <6>
  control   = crumble_control(crossfit_folds = 2L, epochs = 20L)  # <7>
)
```
1. Baseline confounders of the $A \to Y$, $A \to M$, and $M \to Y$ relationships; must all be measured **before** treatment; do not include post-treatment variables here
2. The mediator(s) on the causal pathway from $A$ to $Y$; `crumble` models $P(M \mid A, W)$ and uses it in the Riesz representer estimation
3. `d0` and `d1` are **intervention functions**: they take the data and treatment column name and return the counterfactual treatment value for each observation; `d0` sets everyone to the reference level (treatment = "1", i.e., control), `d1` sets everyone to the active level (treatment = "2")
4. `effect = "N"` requests natural direct and indirect effects (NDE/NIE); requires the cross-world independence assumption (no treatment-induced confounders of $M \to Y$); use `"RI"` or `"RT"` if intermediate confounders exist
5. `learners`: the Super Learner library used for all nuisance models (outcome $E[Y \mid A, M, W]$, mediator $P(M \mid A, W)$, treatment $P(A \mid W)$); `"earth"` (MARS) and `"ranger"` (random forest) capture nonlinear mediator-outcome relationships
6. `nn_module = sequential_module()`: a neural network used to estimate the **Riesz representer**, a density ratio of the mediator distribution under treatment vs. control; this avoids explicit density estimation which is difficult in high dimensions
7. `crumble_control(crossfit_folds = 2L, epochs = 20L)`: controls cross-fitting (2 folds here for speed) and neural network training epochs; increase to `crossfit_folds = 10L, epochs = 100L` for production analyses

# Interpretation

## What do the effects mean in context?

In the HAART example:

- The **direct effect** captures the mortality benefit of HAART that does *not* operate through CD4 recovery: viral suppression acting on tissues other than CD4 lymphocytes, prevention of opportunistic infections through reduced viral burden, and lower systemic inflammation independent of CD4 count. A large direct effect suggests that monitoring HAART by CD4 alone misses an important part of the drug's protective action.

- The **indirect effect** (via M) captures how much of the mortality benefit is attributable to immune reconstitution as measured by CD4 change. If this is large, CD4 trajectory is a strong surrogate for the mortality benefit; if small, CD4 monitoring is not capturing the dominant mechanism.

- The **total effect** is the sum (for additive decompositions). It should be close to what you would estimate from a standard TMLE or IPTW analysis of $A$ on $Y$.

The choice of effect definition matters for interpretation. Natural effects answer "what if we could intervene on the mediator to hold it at its control value?" This is a hypothetical intervention that may not be physically realizable.

Randomized interventional effects answer a different question: "what if we randomly assigned the mediator value from its distribution under control?" This stochastic intervention is closer to policy-relevant questions. Recanting twins decompose the total effect into path-specific components that remain identifiable even with intermediate confounding.

None of these claims establish "mechanism" in a biological sense without strong assumptions. The decomposition tells you how much of the effect flows through the measured mediator *given the causal model and identification assumptions*. Unmeasured mediator-outcome confounders, measurement error in $M$, or a misspecified time ordering can all distort it.


## Caution: proportion mediated

The "proportion mediated" (NIE / TE) is commonly reported but has several problems [@vanderweele2014tutorial]:

- It is **undefined** when the total effect is zero or near zero.
- It can be **greater than 1 or negative** when the direct and indirect effects have opposite signs.
- It is **highly variable** because it is a ratio of two estimated quantities, each with uncertainty.
- It is **scale-dependent**: the proportion mediated on the risk-difference scale differs from that on the odds-ratio scale.

Report the direct and indirect effects on the absolute scale (risk difference) with confidence intervals. If you report proportion mediated, note these caveats.

# What to Report

When publishing a mediation analysis, include:

1. **Effect definition:** Which estimand (natural, interventional, recanting twins) and why.
2. **Time ordering:** When each variable was measured and why the ordering supports mediation claims.
3. **Confounder sets:** Which variables are treated as baseline confounders ($W$), mediators ($M$), and intermediate confounders ($Z$), with justification.
4. **Identification assumptions:** State them explicitly. Note which are testable and which require subject-matter judgment.
5. **Estimator details:** TMLE with cross-fitting, number of folds, learner library.
6. **Point estimates and uncertainty:** Effect estimates on the risk-difference scale with 95% confidence intervals.
7. **Diagnostics:** Propensity score overlap, mediator distribution overlap, density ratio stability.
8. **Sensitivity analysis:** How robust are conclusions to unmeasured confounding or model misspecification?
9. **Limitations:** Measurement error in the mediator, unmeasured pathways, temporal resolution of the data.


# Key Takeaways

::: {.callout-note}
## Summary

1. **Mediation analysis decomposes a total effect** into direct and indirect components. This tells us *how much* of a drug's benefit flows through a specific mechanism, but only under strong assumptions.

2. **The choice of estimand matters.** Natural effects require the cross-world assumption (no treatment-induced mediator-outcome confounders). Randomized interventional effects and recanting twins relax this at the cost of a slightly different interpretation.

3. **Baron-Kenny regression fails** for binary outcomes because of non-collapsibility and post-treatment confounding. Even for continuous outcomes, it assumes linearity and no interactions.

4. **TMLE-based mediation** (via `crumble`) combines flexible nuisance estimation, cross-fitting, and targeting to provide multiply robust estimates (consistent if any two of the outcome model, mediator density, and treatment propensity are correct) with valid inference under appropriate regularity conditions.

5. **Diagnostics are essential:** propensity score overlap, mediator distribution shift, density ratio stability, and the mediator-outcome relationship should all be examined.

6. **Report responsibly.** State the estimand, assumptions, time ordering, and limitations. Avoid over-interpreting "proportion mediated" or claiming biological mechanism without acknowledging the assumptions required.
:::
