What to do when no unmeasured confounding is not credible

Instrumental variables, negative controls, and proximal inference when adjustment is not enough

Estimated reading time: 35 minutes

Every method in this handbook so far rests on the same load-bearing assumption: that the measured covariates are enough to remove confounding, so that treated and untreated patients with the same covariate values are comparable. The Causal Roadmap (Chapter 5) calls it conditional exchangeability, and it is the one assumption the data cannot check. In pharmacoepidemiology it is also the assumption most likely to fail. Sicker patients are treated more aggressively, healthier patients adhere better, and the frailty or disease severity that drives both treatment and outcome is rarely captured fully in claims or records. When adjustment is not credible no matter how rich the covariate set, three strategies let an analysis make progress by trading the no-unmeasured-confounding assumption for a different structural assumption that may be easier to defend in the problem at hand.

Learning objectives
  • Recognize when conditional exchangeability is too weak to support an adjustment-based analysis
  • Explain what an instrumental variable identifies, and what it does not
  • Use negative controls to detect, and begin to correct, residual confounding
  • Understand proximal causal inference as the estimator that negative controls point toward
Scope

This chapter covers identification strategies that relax conditional exchangeability while staying within the targeted-learning and potential-outcomes framework. The classic quasi-experimental designs of econometrics and policy evaluation, difference-in-differences, regression discontinuity, and interrupted time series, solve related problems with different machinery and are pointed to in the further reading rather than developed here.

30.1 1. The assumption you cannot test, and usually cannot trust

Confounding by indication is the standard cautionary example. A drug prescribed to the sickest patients will look harmful in a crude comparison, and look harmful still after adjusting for the severity markers that happen to be recorded, because the part of severity that drove the prescribing decision is never fully measured. The healthy-user effect runs the other way: patients who start a preventive therapy tend to be more health-conscious in ways that also lower their risk, so the therapy looks protective beyond its true effect. In both cases adding more covariates helps only to the extent that the new covariates capture the unmeasured driver, and the analyst has no way to know from the data when enough has been captured.

The strategies below share a common move. Each introduces an extra variable, an instrument or a negative control or a proxy, whose relationship to treatment and outcome is constrained by subject-matter knowledge rather than by the data. That constraint replaces the no-unmeasured-confounding assumption with a new one. The new assumption is still untestable, but it is a different kind of claim, and in the right problem it is far more defensible because it follows from a known mechanism.

30.2 2. Instrumental variables

Sometimes the data contain a hidden coin flip. Which physician a patient happened to see, not how sick they were, tipped the treatment one way or the other, and that accident is what lets us learn the effect. An instrument is a variable that nudges treatment without otherwise touching the outcome. Formally it must satisfy three conditions: it is associated with treatment (relevance); it affects the outcome only through treatment (the exclusion restriction); and it shares no common cause with the outcome (independence). A classic pharmacoepidemiologic instrument is a physician’s or facility’s prescribing preference. Two patients who happen to see a doctor with a strong preference for drug A versus drug B are, on average, exchangeable in their own characteristics, yet they receive different treatments because of the prescriber, so the prescriber’s preference nudges treatment without being caused by the patient’s prognosis. Calendar-time policy changes and, in genetic epidemiology, an inherited variant used in Mendelian randomization, play the same role.

What an instrument buys is identification without measuring the confounder, because the instrument induces variation in treatment that is unrelated to the unmeasured driver. What it costs is twofold and worth stating plainly. First, the estimand changes. Under a monotonicity assumption, an instrument identifies the effect among the patients whose treatment the instrument actually moved, the so-called compliers, not the average effect in the whole population. In the prescribing-preference example, that is the effect among patients whose treatment would differ depending on which physician they happened to see, which is a real and policy-relevant group but not the same as everyone. Second, the exclusion restriction and independence are themselves untestable, and a weak instrument, one only loosely associated with treatment, produces estimates that are both imprecise and biased toward the confounded answer. A credible instrumental-variable analysis therefore leans on a strong, mechanistically justified instrument and reports the local nature of the effect it identifies.

The targeted-learning toolkit extends to instrumental-variable estimands: doubly robust and machine-learning-based estimators of the local effect exist and use the same Super Learner nuisance machinery seen elsewhere in this book. The example below contrasts a naive confounded estimate with a two-stage least-squares instrumental-variable estimate on simulated data and is marked not to run.

Show code 
set.seed(2026)
n  <- 4000
U  <- rnorm(n)                                   # unmeasured confounder (severity)
Z  <- rbinom(n, 1, 0.5)                          # instrument: prescriber preference
A  <- rbinom(n, 1, plogis(-0.2 + 1.3 * Z + 0.9 * U))   # treatment depends on Z and U
Y  <- 0.5 + 0.0 * A + 1.0 * U + rnorm(n)         # TRUE treatment effect is 0; U confounds

# Naive: regress outcome on treatment, ignoring U (biased by confounding)
naive <- coef(lm(Y ~ A))["A"]

# Instrumental-variable (two-stage least squares) estimate of the effect
fs   <- lm(A ~ Z)                                # stage 1: treatment on instrument
Ahat <- predict(fs)
iv   <- coef(lm(Y ~ Ahat))["Ahat"]               # stage 2: outcome on predicted treatment

c(naive = round(naive, 3), iv = round(iv, 3))    # naive far from 0; IV near 0

The naive coefficient picks up the confounding through U and points to an effect that is not there, while the instrumental-variable estimate, using only the variation in treatment that the instrument induced, recovers the true null. That is the whole appeal of the design, achieved without ever measuring U.

30.3 3. Negative controls

A negative control is a variable chosen so that one of its causal links is known to be absent, which lets it act as a tripwire for confounding. A negative control outcome is an outcome the treatment cannot plausibly cause, yet which shares the confounding structure of the real outcome. A negative control exposure is an exposure that cannot plausibly cause the real outcome, yet which shares the same confounders. If a careful analysis finds a treatment effect on a negative control outcome, or an effect of the negative control exposure on the real outcome, the most likely explanation is residual confounding, because the causal link that would explain it is known to be absent.

The canonical example comes from influenza-vaccine studies. Vaccinated older adults appeared to have much lower all-cause mortality than unvaccinated peers, including before the influenza season had begun. Death in the pre-season period is a negative control outcome: the vaccine cannot prevent deaths from influenza that has not yet circulated, so the apparent benefit in that window had to be confounding, the healthy-vaccinee effect, and it called the headline estimate into question. The NSAID-and-fracture check in Chapter 19 is the same idea applied as a diagnostic.

Used this way a negative control is a falsification test: it can reveal bias but does not by itself remove it. The value of a clean negative control is real, because an analysis that passes a well-chosen falsification test is more credible than one that was never challenged. The next strategy turns the negative control from a test into an estimator.

30.4 4. Proximal causal inference

Proximal causal inference starts from a simple observation. If a negative control carries information about the unmeasured confounder, then with enough negative controls of the right kinds it should be possible not just to detect the bias but to remove it. The setup uses two proxies of the unmeasured confounder: one connected to the treatment side, sometimes called a treatment-inducing proxy, and one connected to the outcome side, which is essentially a negative control outcome. Neither proxy measures the confounder directly, and neither alone is enough. Used together, under conditions that link them to the hidden confounder, they identify the causal effect through a pair of equations, called bridge functions, that play the role the unmeasured confounder would have played if it had been observed.

The intuition is worth holding onto even without the equations. Two imperfect, noisy measurements of the same hidden variable can, in combination, stand in for that variable in a way that neither could alone, much as two angles fix a position that one angle leaves ambiguous. A concrete pharmacoepidemiologic instance: baseline disease severity is unmeasured, but a pre-treatment lab value related to how aggressively a clinician treats serves as the treatment-side proxy, while a pre-treatment symptom score related to prognosis serves as the outcome-side proxy. Proximal methods combine the two to recover the effect that adjustment alone could not.

Proximal inference is the most technically demanding of the three strategies and is an active research area. It requires genuinely valid proxies and additional completeness conditions that are themselves untestable, and the estimators, including targeted-learning versions, are still maturing in available software. The practical posture for now is to understand it as the principled destination that negative controls point toward, to collect candidate proxies prospectively when a study is being designed, and to consult the current methodological literature before attempting an applied proximal analysis.

30.5 5. Choosing a strategy, and how each one fails

These three strategies do not compete so much as suit different situations, and each fails in a characteristic way. An instrumental-variable analysis lives or dies on the instrument: a strong, mechanistically justified instrument such as a genuine prescribing preference can be compelling, while a weak or arguable one delivers an estimate that is imprecise and quietly biased back toward the confounded answer, and even a perfect instrument identifies only the effect among the patients it moved. A negative-control analysis depends on finding a control that truly shares the confounding structure while being genuinely unaffected by the causal link in question; a poorly chosen control either misses real bias or raises a false alarm. Proximal inference depends on two valid proxies and conditions that are hard to verify, and on software and expertise that are still developing.

The common thread is that none of these strategies escapes untestable assumptions; they relocate them. The reason to reach for one is that its assumption follows from something you know about how the data were generated, a known prescribing mechanism, a biologically impossible link, a measured proxy for a known confounder, rather than from the hope that the covariate list is complete. When that substantive justification is absent, the honest move is a sensitivity analysis (Chapter 19) that reports how strong an unmeasured confounder would have to be to overturn the result, not a switch to a design whose own assumptions are no more defensible.

30.8 Check your understanding

Q1. Why does an instrumental-variable analysis usually estimate something other than the average treatment effect?

Under the standard monotonicity assumption, an instrument identifies the effect only among the patients whose treatment the instrument actually changed, the compliers. Patients who would take the same treatment regardless of the instrument contribute no information about the effect, so the estimate is local to the moved subgroup. This complier effect is real and often policy-relevant, but it is not the population average effect and should be reported as what it is.

Q2. A reviewer asks you to add a negative control outcome to a study of a drug and stroke. What makes a good choice, and what does a non-null result mean?

A good negative control outcome is something the drug cannot plausibly cause within the study window but that is subject to the same confounding as stroke, for example an outcome driven by the same frailty and healthcare-seeking behavior. If the analysis estimates a non-null effect on that control, the causal link that would explain it is known to be absent, so the finding points to residual confounding biasing the main estimate. It detects the problem; it does not by itself fix it.

Q3. In one sentence, how does proximal causal inference go beyond a negative control?

A negative control uses one proxy of the unmeasured confounder to test for bias, whereas proximal inference combines a treatment-side proxy and an outcome-side proxy to actually identify and remove the effect of the unmeasured confounder, turning the diagnostic into an estimator.
